# Executive Summary

The Standard Delivery Lifecycle defines the operational sequence through which
Cognitive Logic Enterprise Services are initiated, governed, executed, validated,
reported and continuously improved.

It translates the principles established by DF-001 — Enterprise Delivery
Methodology into a repeatable delivery process applicable across the Enterprise
Service Catalogue.

The lifecycle establishes ten governed phases:

1. Customer Engagement
2. Discovery
3. Evidence Collection
4. Assessment
5. Analysis
6. Recommendation Design
7. Executive Validation
8. Executive Reporting
9. Delivery
10. Continuous Improvement

Each phase has a defined purpose, accountable roles, required inputs, controlled
activities, mandatory outputs, measurable performance indicators, evidence
requirements and completion criteria.

Progression between phases is controlled through quality gates, decision gates
and governance checkpoints.

No phase is considered complete solely because its planned activities have been
performed.

Completion requires demonstrable evidence that:

- the required outputs exist;
- the outputs meet the applicable quality criteria;
- material assumptions have been documented;
- decisions are traceable;
- risks and exceptions have been governed;
- accountable stakeholders have completed the required validation;
- the delivery record is sufficiently complete to support audit and reuse.

The lifecycle is technology-independent, vendor-independent and AI-provider
independent.

Operational conclusions and recommendations must be grounded in intelligible
data, governed knowledge, measurable indicators and verifiable evidence.

# Purpose

The purpose of DF-002 is to establish the authoritative operational standard for
the delivery of Cognitive Logic Enterprise Services.

The document provides a common execution model that enables delivery teams to:

- initiate engagements consistently;
- define delivery boundaries;
- establish decision rights;
- collect and govern evidence;
- perform reproducible assessments;
- separate findings from interpretation;
- design actionable recommendations;
- validate conclusions with accountable stakeholders;
- produce executive-grade reporting;
- complete controlled delivery handover;
- establish mechanisms for continuous improvement.

DF-002 is intended to reduce variation between engagements without eliminating
the professional judgement required for complex enterprise environments.

It provides the minimum lifecycle requirements that every Enterprise Service
must satisfy while allowing service-specific procedures, evidence models,
assessment instruments and deliverables to be applied where necessary.

The lifecycle also ensures that delivery activity can be reconstructed after
completion through a coherent chain connecting inputs, evidence, analysis,
decisions, recommendations, approvals and final outputs.

# Lifecycle Overview

The Standard Delivery Lifecycle is a controlled sequence rather than a purely
linear project plan.

The ten phases define the normal progression of an engagement, but controlled
iteration may occur when evidence, validation or governance findings require a
return to an earlier phase.

A phase may begin only when its minimum entry conditions are satisfied.

A phase may close only when:

- mandatory activities have been completed;
- mandatory deliverables have been produced;
- required evidence has been registered;
- applicable KPI have been measured;
- material risks have been addressed or accepted;
- the phase quality gate has been passed;
- the responsible decision authority has authorised progression.

The lifecycle uses four control mechanisms:

1. entry criteria;
2. phase completion criteria;
3. quality gates;
4. decision gates.

Entry criteria confirm that the engagement is ready to begin a phase.

Completion criteria confirm that operational work has reached the required
state.

Quality gates verify the integrity, completeness, consistency and usability of
phase outputs.

Decision gates establish whether accountable stakeholders authorise
progression, remediation, escalation, suspension or termination.

The lifecycle must remain proportionate to the size, risk, complexity and
strategic relevance of the engagement.

Proportionality may change the depth of execution, but it must not remove:

- evidence traceability;
- accountable ownership;
- documented decisions;
- minimum quality controls;
- completion criteria;
- required approvals.

# Delivery Governance

Delivery governance establishes the authority, accountability and control
structure through which the lifecycle is executed.

Every engagement must identify at minimum:

- an Executive Sponsor;
- a Customer Project Lead;
- a Cognitive Logic Delivery Lead;
- relevant Subject Matter Experts;
- required Executive Stakeholders;
- phase-specific decision authorities.

The Cognitive Logic Delivery Lead is accountable for lifecycle integrity.

This accountability includes ensuring that:

- phases are initiated under valid entry conditions;
- required activities are performed;
- evidence is registered and traceable;
- deliverables follow approved standards;
- quality gates are executed;
- unresolved risks are escalated;
- decisions are documented;
- phase closure is formally recorded.

The Executive Sponsor retains authority over strategic direction, customer
commitment, material scope changes and acceptance of significant residual risk.

The Customer Project Lead coordinates customer participation, access to
information, stakeholder availability and operational validation.

Subject Matter Experts contribute domain knowledge but do not replace formal
decision authorities.

Governance must distinguish between:

- preparation;
- review;
- recommendation;
- validation;
- approval;
- acceptance.

The individual or role that prepares an output should not be treated
automatically as the authority that approves it.

Material decisions must identify:

- the decision subject;
- the available options;
- the supporting evidence;
- the accountable decision-maker;
- the decision date;
- the selected outcome;
- relevant conditions;
- unresolved risks;
- required follow-up actions.

Lifecycle governance must be supported by a maintained delivery record.

The delivery record is the authoritative operational index connecting the
engagement scope, stakeholders, evidence, assessments, findings, recommendations,
decisions, deliverables, quality controls and approvals.

No external AI provider may act as a delivery decision authority, evidence
authority or source of unverified recommendations.

The Sovereign Intelligence Architecture may support governed analysis through:

- QEN Sovereign Engine;
- Governance Engine;
- Knowledge Graph;
- EVIDE;
- proprietary algorithms;
- intelligible data;
- verifiable evidence.

Human accountability remains mandatory for all material delivery decisions.

# Phase 1 — Customer Engagement

## Objective

Customer Engagement establishes the controlled foundation of the delivery
relationship.

The phase confirms that the proposed engagement is sufficiently understood,
authorised, feasible and aligned with the applicable Enterprise Service
specification before substantive delivery activity begins.

## Entry Conditions

The phase may begin when:

- a qualified customer need has been identified;
- the relevant Enterprise Service has been selected or provisionally identified;
- an accountable customer contact is available;
- initial commercial and operational boundaries are known;
- no unresolved condition prevents formal engagement initiation.

## Activities

Mandatory activities include:

- confirming the customer organisation and engagement context;
- identifying the requested service and expected business outcome;
- clarifying the initial problem statement;
- identifying executive and operational stakeholders;
- defining preliminary scope boundaries;
- identifying known constraints and dependencies;
- establishing communication and escalation channels;
- confirming confidentiality and information-handling requirements;
- recording initial assumptions;
- identifying material conflicts of interest;
- confirming engagement readiness.

The phase must distinguish between the customer’s stated request and the
underlying governance, knowledge or decision problem.

Unvalidated customer assumptions must not be treated as established facts.

## Inputs

Required inputs include:

- customer request;
- selected Enterprise Service specification;
- preliminary organisational context;
- available commercial documentation;
- known stakeholder information;
- known regulatory, strategic or operational constraints;
- relevant prior engagement records, where applicable.

## Outputs

Required outputs include:

- engagement brief;
- preliminary problem statement;
- initial scope statement;
- stakeholder register;
- initial assumptions register;
- initial constraints and dependencies register;
- communication model;
- preliminary risk record;
- engagement readiness decision.

## Mandatory Deliverables

The mandatory phase deliverables are:

1. Engagement Brief;
2. Preliminary Scope Statement;
3. Stakeholder Register;
4. Initial Assumptions and Constraints Register;
5. Engagement Readiness Record.

## Responsibilities

The Executive Sponsor confirms strategic relevance and organisational
commitment.

The Customer Project Lead coordinates access to stakeholders and preliminary
information.

The Cognitive Logic Delivery Lead:

- validates lifecycle applicability;
- confirms the relevant service specification;
- establishes the initial delivery record;
- identifies missing prerequisites;
- records assumptions, constraints and risks;
- prepares the engagement readiness decision.

Subject Matter Experts may support preliminary feasibility analysis where the
engagement involves specialised domains.

## Decisions

The principal decision is whether the engagement may proceed to Discovery.

Permitted outcomes are:

- proceed;
- proceed with conditions;
- return for clarification;
- escalate;
- suspend;
- decline.

Conditional progression must identify the unresolved condition, accountable
owner and resolution deadline.

## KPI

Minimum phase KPI include:

- percentage of mandatory stakeholders identified;
- percentage of required engagement fields completed;
- number of unresolved entry conditions;
- number of undocumented assumptions;
- elapsed time from qualified request to readiness decision;
- stakeholder availability rate;
- engagement readiness completeness score.

## Evidence Required

Required evidence includes:

- source of the customer request;
- service selection rationale;
- stakeholder confirmations;
- documented scope assumptions;
- known constraints;
- readiness review outcome;
- approval or conditional approval record.

## Completion Criteria

The phase is complete when:

- the engagement objective is intelligible;
- the service boundary is sufficiently defined;
- accountable stakeholders have been identified;
- initial risks and assumptions are recorded;
- communication and escalation channels are established;
- prerequisites have been verified or conditionally accepted;
- the Engagement Readiness Record has been approved.

## Dependencies

Dependencies may include:

- commercial approval;
- contractual readiness;
- stakeholder availability;
- access permissions;
- confidentiality arrangements;
- customer sponsorship;
- service-specific prerequisites.

## Risks

Typical risks include:

- ambiguous customer objectives;
- incorrect service selection;
- unavailable decision-makers;
- unrealistic expectations;
- hidden dependencies;
- incomplete disclosure of constraints;
- premature commitment to solutions;
- confusion between advisory and legal responsibility.

## Quality Gate

The Customer Engagement Quality Gate verifies that:

- the engagement is authorised;
- the problem statement is sufficiently clear;
- scope boundaries are documented;
- stakeholders and decision authorities are identified;
- known assumptions and constraints are visible;
- unresolved conditions are governed;
- the delivery record has been initiated.

Failure of the quality gate prevents progression to Discovery.

# Phase 2 — Discovery

## Objective

Discovery develops a shared, evidence-oriented understanding of the customer
environment, business problem, governance context, decision requirements and
expected outcomes.

The phase converts the preliminary engagement brief into an approved discovery
baseline suitable for structured evidence collection and assessment.

## Entry Conditions

Discovery may begin when:

- the Customer Engagement Quality Gate has passed;
- the Engagement Readiness Record exists;
- the Customer Project Lead has confirmed stakeholder access;
- the preliminary scope has been accepted;
- material confidentiality requirements have been addressed.

## Activities

Mandatory activities include:

- conducting structured executive and operational interviews;
- examining the organisational context;
- identifying business capabilities affected by the engagement;
- mapping relevant processes, systems, information assets and decision flows;
- identifying governance structures and decision rights;
- clarifying expected outcomes and success conditions;
- identifying evidence sources;
- detecting information gaps;
- identifying regulatory and policy dependencies;
- refining scope and exclusions;
- validating terminology and definitions;
- documenting material areas of disagreement.

Discovery must separate:

- stated objectives from measurable outcomes;
- formal governance from actual practice;
- documented processes from observed processes;
- available evidence from claimed evidence;
- current state from desired state.

## Inputs

Required inputs include:

- Engagement Brief;
- Preliminary Scope Statement;
- Stakeholder Register;
- assumptions and constraints;
- relevant organisational documentation;
- service-specific discovery instruments;
- prior assessments, where available;
- applicable policies, procedures and governance records.

## Outputs

Required outputs include:

- discovery record;
- validated problem statement;
- current-state context map;
- decision and stakeholder map;
- refined scope;
- evidence source inventory;
- information gap register;
- discovery risk update;
- approved discovery baseline.

## Mandatory Deliverables

The mandatory phase deliverables are:

1. Discovery Record;
2. Validated Problem Statement;
3. Current-State Context Map;
4. Evidence Source Inventory;
5. Information Gap Register;
6. Discovery Baseline Approval Record.

## Responsibilities

The Cognitive Logic Delivery Lead governs the discovery process and ensures
consistency between interviews, documentation and recorded evidence.

The Customer Project Lead:

- schedules stakeholder participation;
- facilitates access to documentation;
- validates organisational context;
- escalates access limitations.

The Executive Sponsor validates strategic objectives and material scope
decisions.

Subject Matter Experts validate specialised terminology, processes and domain
constraints.

## Decisions

The principal decision is whether the discovery baseline is sufficient to begin
Evidence Collection.

The decision must consider:

- clarity of the problem statement;
- adequacy of stakeholder coverage;
- availability of evidence sources;
- material information gaps;
- scope stability;
- identified risks;
- feasibility of the planned assessment.

## KPI

Minimum phase KPI include:

- stakeholder interview completion rate;
- planned versus completed discovery sessions;
- evidence source identification rate;
- number of material information gaps;
- percentage of scope elements validated;
- number of unresolved terminology conflicts;
- discovery baseline completeness score;
- stakeholder validation rate.

## Evidence Required

Required evidence includes:

- interview records;
- validated meeting notes;
- reviewed organisational documents;
- context maps;
- identified evidence sources;
- documented information gaps;
- stakeholder comments;
- scope approval records.

## Completion Criteria

The phase is complete when:

- the problem statement has been validated;
- the current-state context is sufficiently understood;
- material stakeholders have participated;
- evidence sources have been identified;
- information gaps are documented;
- scope and exclusions are approved;
- the Discovery Baseline Approval Record is complete.

## Dependencies

Dependencies may include:

- executive and operational stakeholder access;
- availability of organisational documentation;
- access to policies, procedures and process owners;
- service-specific discovery instruments;
- availability of specialised expertise;
- resolution of confidentiality restrictions.

## Risks

Typical risks include:

- stakeholder bias;
- incomplete stakeholder representation;
- undocumented operational practices;
- inconsistent terminology;
- reliance on unverified statements;
- discovery scope expansion;
- restricted evidence access;
- failure to distinguish formal controls from actual controls.

## Quality Gate

The Discovery Quality Gate verifies that:

- the validated problem statement is clear;
- scope and exclusions are approved;
- the organisational context is intelligible;
- stakeholder coverage is adequate;
- evidence sources are identifiable;
- material gaps and risks are documented;
- the discovery baseline supports structured Evidence Collection.

Failure of the quality gate requires remediation, scope revision or formal
escalation.

# Phase 3 — Evidence Collection

## Objective

Evidence Collection establishes the governed evidentiary foundation required for
assessment, analysis, recommendation design and executive decision-making.

The phase ensures that relevant information is acquired, classified, validated,
registered and made traceable before it is used to support findings or
recommendations.

## Entry Conditions

Evidence Collection may begin when:

- the Discovery Quality Gate has passed;
- the Discovery Baseline Approval Record exists;
- the Evidence Source Inventory has been approved;
- required access permissions are available;
- evidence handling requirements have been defined;
- material information gaps are known.

## Activities

Mandatory activities include:

- confirming the evidence collection plan;
- identifying evidence owners and custodians;
- acquiring documentary, operational and system-generated evidence;
- recording evidence origin and acquisition date;
- classifying evidence by type, relevance and reliability;
- validating evidence integrity;
- identifying duplicate, obsolete or conflicting evidence;
- recording evidence limitations;
- linking evidence to scope elements and assessment criteria;
- maintaining chain of custody where required;
- documenting inaccessible or unavailable evidence;
- updating the information gap register;
- obtaining evidence-owner validation where necessary.

Evidence Collection must distinguish between:

- verified evidence;
- unverified information;
- stakeholder assertions;
- inferred information;
- assumptions;
- external reference material;
- derived analytical artefacts.

Unverified information may support further inquiry but must not be represented as
verified evidence.

## Evidence Categories

Evidence may include:

- policies;
- procedures;
- governance records;
- decision records;
- organisational charts;
- risk registers;
- control documentation;
- process maps;
- system inventories;
- data inventories;
- knowledge assets;
- contracts;
- training records;
- audit records;
- regulatory documentation;
- operational metrics;
- interview evidence;
- observation records;
- technical outputs;
- historical reports.

Each evidence item must be assigned an evidence category consistent with the
engagement scope and service specification.

## Inputs

Required inputs include:

- approved Discovery Baseline;
- Evidence Source Inventory;
- Information Gap Register;
- service-specific evidence requirements;
- stakeholder and evidence-owner register;
- evidence handling requirements;
- applicable assessment criteria;
- approved access permissions.

## Outputs

Required outputs include:

- evidence register;
- evidence repository or controlled evidence index;
- evidence classification record;
- evidence reliability assessment;
- evidence gap update;
- evidence conflict record;
- evidence traceability links;
- evidence collection completion record.

## Mandatory Deliverables

The mandatory phase deliverables are:

1. Evidence Collection Plan;
2. Evidence Register;
3. Evidence Classification and Reliability Record;
4. Evidence Gap and Conflict Register;
5. Evidence Traceability Matrix;
6. Evidence Collection Completion Record.

## Responsibilities

The Cognitive Logic Delivery Lead governs the evidence collection process and
ensures compliance with approved evidentiary standards.

Evidence owners are responsible for:

- providing authorised access;
- confirming evidence origin;
- validating contextual accuracy;
- identifying restrictions;
- reporting known limitations.

The Customer Project Lead coordinates evidence availability and resolves
operational access constraints.

Subject Matter Experts support interpretation of specialised evidence but must
not alter original evidence records.

## Evidence Reliability

Evidence reliability must be evaluated using criteria that include:

- authenticity;
- provenance;
- completeness;
- currency;
- consistency;
- relevance;
- independence;
- reproducibility;
- contextual accuracy.

Evidence reliability may be classified as:

- verified;
- conditionally verified;
- partially verified;
- unverified;
- disputed;
- unavailable.

The reliability classification must remain visible throughout the lifecycle.

## Decisions

The principal decision is whether the collected evidence is sufficient,
reliable and representative enough to support Assessment.

Permitted outcomes are:

- evidence sufficient;
- evidence sufficient with limitations;
- targeted collection required;
- scope adjustment required;
- escalation required;
- assessment suspended.

Any decision to proceed with evidence limitations must identify:

- the affected scope area;
- the missing or weak evidence;
- the potential analytical impact;
- the accountable approver;
- required mitigation.

## KPI

Minimum phase KPI include:

- evidence collection completion rate;
- evidence owner response rate;
- percentage of evidence items with verified provenance;
- percentage of evidence classified as verified;
- number of unresolved evidence conflicts;
- number of material evidence gaps;
- evidence traceability coverage;
- average evidence acquisition time;
- percentage of assessment criteria supported by evidence;
- evidence repository completeness score.

## Evidence Required

Evidence required to close the phase includes:

- approved Evidence Collection Plan;
- completed Evidence Register;
- provenance records;
- evidence-owner confirmations;
- reliability classifications;
- evidence gap records;
- evidence conflict records;
- traceability links;
- collection completion approval.

## Completion Criteria

The phase is complete when:

- planned evidence sources have been addressed;
- acquired evidence has been registered;
- provenance and ownership are recorded;
- evidence reliability has been assessed;
- material conflicts are documented;
- evidence gaps are visible;
- evidence is linked to relevant assessment criteria;
- limitations are approved;
- the Evidence Collection Completion Record is complete.

## Dependencies

Dependencies may include:

- evidence-owner cooperation;
- access permissions;
- confidentiality and security requirements;
- data retention policies;
- technical extraction capability;
- legal or contractual restrictions;
- availability of historical evidence;
- service-specific evidence models.

## Risks

Typical risks include:

- incomplete evidence;
- outdated evidence;
- inaccessible evidence;
- undocumented evidence provenance;
- evidence tampering;
- selective disclosure;
- excessive reliance on interviews;
- conflicting records;
- evidence misclassification;
- loss of contextual meaning;
- uncontrolled copies;
- privacy or confidentiality breaches.

## Quality Gate

The Evidence Collection Quality Gate verifies that:

- evidence acquisition is sufficiently complete;
- evidence provenance is documented;
- reliability classifications are assigned;
- material evidence gaps are visible;
- conflicting evidence is governed;
- traceability to assessment criteria exists;
- evidence limitations are formally accepted where applicable;
- the evidentiary foundation is adequate for Assessment.

Failure of the quality gate requires targeted evidence collection, scope
adjustment, escalation or suspension.

# Phase 4 — Assessment

## Objective

Assessment applies approved criteria, models and measurement methods to the
governed evidence base in order to establish a reproducible view of the current
state.

The phase converts evidence into structured findings without prematurely
introducing strategic recommendations.

## Entry Conditions

Assessment may begin when:

- the Evidence Collection Quality Gate has passed;
- the Evidence Collection Completion Record exists;
- assessment criteria have been approved;
- evidence limitations are documented;
- required assessment instruments are available;
- assessor roles and review responsibilities are assigned.

## Activities

Mandatory activities include:

- confirming the assessment model;
- mapping evidence to assessment criteria;
- evaluating the current state;
- applying approved scoring or classification methods;
- recording criterion-level findings;
- identifying control strengths and weaknesses;
- identifying maturity, readiness, risk or capability gaps;
- documenting assessor judgements;
- recording evidence limitations affecting scores;
- performing consistency checks;
- conducting independent review where required;
- resolving or documenting assessment disagreements;
- preparing the assessment results baseline.

Assessment must separate:

- evidence;
- criterion;
- finding;
- score;
- interpretation;
- recommendation.

Recommendations must not be finalised during this phase.

## Assessment Principles

Assessment execution must be:

- evidence-based;
- criterion-driven;
- repeatable;
- explainable;
- proportionate;
- documented;
- reviewable;
- independent from external AI-provider judgement.

Scoring models must identify:

- scale;
- thresholds;
- weighting;
- calculation method;
- treatment of missing evidence;
- treatment of disputed evidence;
- confidence level;
- rounding rules;
- aggregation rules.

No score may be presented without an intelligible explanation of how it was
derived.

## Inputs

Required inputs include:

- approved evidence base;
- Evidence Traceability Matrix;
- service-specific assessment model;
- assessment criteria;
- scoring methodology;
- evidence reliability classifications;
- documented scope and exclusions;
- approved assumptions and limitations.

## Outputs

Required outputs include:

- criterion-level assessment record;
- current-state findings;
- scores and classifications;
- confidence assessments;
- identified gaps;
- assessment exceptions;
- assessment review record;
- approved assessment baseline.

## Mandatory Deliverables

The mandatory phase deliverables are:

1. Assessment Plan;
2. Criterion-Level Assessment Record;
3. Findings Register;
4. Scoring and Classification Record;
5. Assessment Confidence Record;
6. Assessment Review Record;
7. Assessment Baseline Approval Record.

## Responsibilities

The Cognitive Logic Delivery Lead ensures that the assessment model is applied
consistently and that lifecycle controls are maintained.

Assessors are responsible for:

- applying criteria correctly;
- linking findings to evidence;
- documenting judgements;
- recording limitations;
- avoiding unsupported conclusions.

Reviewers are responsible for:

- checking consistency;
- verifying traceability;
- challenging unsupported scores;
- identifying methodological deviation;
- confirming reproducibility.

The Customer Project Lead may validate factual context but must not alter
assessment outcomes without documented evidence.

## Decisions

The principal decision is whether the assessment results are sufficiently
complete, reliable and reproducible to proceed to Analysis.

Permitted outcomes are:

- assessment approved;
- assessment approved with limitations;
- reassessment required;
- additional evidence required;
- methodological review required;
- escalation required.

Any material change to a score or finding must record:

- original result;
- proposed change;
- supporting rationale;
- supporting evidence;
- reviewer;
- approving authority;
- final result.

## KPI

Minimum phase KPI include:

- percentage of criteria assessed;
- percentage of findings linked to evidence;
- number of unsupported findings;
- number of scoring exceptions;
- inter-reviewer consistency rate;
- percentage of criteria with confidence rating;
- number of unresolved assessment disagreements;
- reassessment rate;
- assessment completion time;
- assessment traceability score;
- assessment reproducibility score.

## Evidence Required

Evidence required to close the phase includes:

- approved assessment model;
- completed criterion-level records;
- finding-to-evidence links;
- scoring calculations;
- confidence classifications;
- reviewer comments;
- resolved disagreement records;
- assessment baseline approval.

## Completion Criteria

The phase is complete when:

- all applicable criteria have been assessed;
- findings are linked to evidence;
- scores and classifications are reproducible;
- confidence levels are documented;
- evidence limitations are reflected in the results;
- material disagreements are resolved or escalated;
- independent review is complete where required;
- the Assessment Baseline Approval Record is approved.

## Dependencies

Dependencies may include:

- completeness of the evidence base;
- availability of approved assessment models;
- assessor competence;
- reviewer independence;
- service-specific scoring rules;
- regulatory criteria;
- customer validation of factual context.

## Risks

Typical risks include:

- assessor bias;
- inconsistent scoring;
- unsupported findings;
- inappropriate weighting;
- hidden assumptions;
- misuse of incomplete evidence;
- false precision;
- scope drift;
- conflation of current state and target state;
- premature recommendation design;
- excessive dependence on stakeholder opinion.

## Quality Gate

The Assessment Quality Gate verifies that:

- the approved methodology has been followed;
- applicable criteria have been assessed;
- findings are evidence-linked;
- scoring is intelligible and reproducible;
- confidence levels are documented;
- limitations and exceptions are visible;
- review controls have been completed;
- the assessment baseline is suitable for Analysis.

Failure of the quality gate requires reassessment, evidence remediation,
methodological review or formal escalation.

# Phase 5 — Analysis

## Objective

Analysis interprets the approved assessment baseline in order to identify
material patterns, causal relationships, systemic weaknesses, dependencies,
risks, opportunities and decision-relevant implications.

The phase transforms structured findings into governed analytical conclusions
without yet converting them into final recommendations.

## Entry Conditions

Analysis may begin when:

- the Assessment Quality Gate has passed;
- the Assessment Baseline Approval Record exists;
- material findings are linked to evidence;
- confidence levels and limitations are documented;
- unresolved assessment exceptions are visible;
- analytical responsibilities are assigned.

## Activities

Mandatory activities include:

- reviewing the approved assessment baseline;
- grouping related findings;
- identifying recurring patterns;
- analysing root causes;
- identifying direct and indirect dependencies;
- evaluating organisational and governance implications;
- distinguishing symptoms from structural causes;
- analysing risk concentration;
- identifying capability and maturity gaps;
- identifying decision bottlenecks;
- identifying knowledge and evidence weaknesses;
- analysing inconsistencies between policy and practice;
- evaluating the impact of evidence limitations;
- developing analytical hypotheses;
- testing hypotheses against available evidence;
- documenting alternative interpretations;
- recording residual uncertainty;
- preparing prioritised analytical conclusions.

Analysis must distinguish between:

- observed fact;
- assessed finding;
- correlation;
- inferred relationship;
- causal conclusion;
- analytical hypothesis;
- professional judgement;
- unresolved uncertainty.

No causal statement may be presented as established unless supported by
sufficient evidence and an intelligible analytical rationale.

## Analytical Dimensions

Analysis may consider:

- governance maturity;
- decision effectiveness;
- accountability;
- evidence quality;
- knowledge governance;
- process coherence;
- control effectiveness;
- regulatory readiness;
- organisational capability;
- data intelligibility;
- explainability;
- decision traceability;
- operational resilience;
- stakeholder alignment;
- implementation feasibility.

The applicable analytical dimensions must derive from the service specification
and approved engagement scope.

## Inputs

Required inputs include:

- approved Assessment Baseline;
- Findings Register;
- Scoring and Classification Record;
- Assessment Confidence Record;
- Evidence Traceability Matrix;
- documented assumptions and limitations;
- applicable risk and maturity models;
- service-specific analytical criteria.

## Entry Conditions

Continuous Improvement may begin when:

- the Delivery Quality Gate has passed;
- the Lifecycle Closure or Transition Record exists;
- improvement ownership has been assigned;
- applicable follow-up periods are defined;
- KPI and success measures are available;
- confidentiality and reuse boundaries are documented.

## Activities

Mandatory activities include:

- reviewing delivery performance;
- evaluating phase KPI;
- comparing expected and observed outcomes;
- reviewing recommendation implementation status;
- identifying recurring delivery issues;
- reviewing customer feedback;
- analysing quality gate failures and exceptions;
- reviewing unresolved and residual risks;
- identifying methodological improvements;
- identifying service specification improvements;
- identifying reusable knowledge;
- recording lessons learned;
- prioritising improvement actions;
- assigning improvement owners;
- defining validation measures;
- updating approved operational assets where authorised;
- escalating changes requiring architectural governance.

Continuous Improvement must distinguish between:

- customer-specific improvement;
- engagement process improvement;
- service specification improvement;
- framework-level improvement;
- architectural change;
- editorial correction;
- training need;
- control remediation.

Framework-level or architectural changes must not be introduced informally.

They require the applicable governance, review and Architecture Decision Record
process.

## Improvement Sources

Improvement sources may include:

- lifecycle KPI;
- customer feedback;
- quality gate records;
- decision gate records;
- delivery exceptions;
- risk events;
- evidence gaps;
- assessment disagreements;
- reporting defects;
- implementation outcomes;
- audit findings;
- regulatory developments;
- service performance trends;
- internal methodological reviews.

External information may inform contextual awareness but must not override
governed internal evidence and approved decision processes.

## Inputs

Required inputs include:

- Lifecycle Closure or Transition Record;
- phase KPI records;
- quality gate outcomes;
- decision gate outcomes;
- customer feedback;
- unresolved issue register;
- implementation action status;
- residual risk records;
- delivery review notes;
- applicable service and framework baselines.

## Outputs

Required outputs include:

- lessons learned register;
- improvement opportunity register;
- prioritised improvement backlog;
- improvement ownership record;
- delivery performance review;
- service improvement proposals;
- framework improvement proposals;
- architectural change proposals where required;
- continuous improvement review record.

## Mandatory Deliverables

The mandatory phase deliverables are:

1. Delivery Performance Review;
2. Lessons Learned Register;
3. Improvement Opportunity Register;
4. Prioritised Improvement Backlog;
5. Improvement Ownership Record;
6. Service or Framework Change Proposal, where applicable;
7. Continuous Improvement Review Record.

## Responsibilities

The Cognitive Logic Delivery Lead is responsible for:

- initiating the delivery review;
- consolidating KPI;
- documenting lessons learned;
- identifying methodological issues;
- protecting customer confidentiality;
- routing changes through the correct governance mechanism.

The Customer Project Lead provides implementation feedback and coordinates
customer-specific improvement actions.

Recommendation owners provide status and outcome information for assigned
actions.

Framework owners evaluate proposed changes affecting:

- lifecycle standards;
- templates;
- quality controls;
- evidence requirements;
- KPI;
- governance checkpoints;
- role definitions.

Enterprise Architecture Governance evaluates changes affecting approved
architecture, methodology or sovereign intelligence principles.

## Improvement Prioritisation

Improvement actions should be prioritised according to:

- impact;
- recurrence;
- severity;
- strategic relevance;
- customer value;
- delivery risk reduction;
- evidence quality improvement;
- implementation feasibility;
- urgency;
- dependency;
- reuse potential;
- architectural significance.

The prioritisation method must remain documented and reproducible.

## Decisions

Continuous Improvement decisions may include:

- close without further action;
- implement customer-specific improvement;
- update an operational procedure;
- update a template;
- propose a service specification revision;
- propose a Delivery Framework revision;
- initiate training;
- introduce an additional control;
- initiate an Architecture Decision Record;
- defer improvement;
- reject improvement.

Each decision must record its rationale, owner, status and validation method.

## KPI

Minimum phase KPI include:

- percentage of phases reviewed;
- percentage of lifecycle KPI evaluated;
- number of lessons learned recorded;
- number of improvement opportunities identified;
- percentage of improvement actions assigned;
- percentage of improvement actions completed;
- recurrence rate of previously identified issues;
- customer feedback response rate;
- recommendation implementation visibility;
- quality gate failure trend;
- average improvement closure time;
- service reuse rate of approved improvements;
- continuous improvement completeness score.

## Evidence Required

Evidence required to close a review cycle includes:

- delivery performance data;
- KPI results;
- customer feedback;
- implementation status;
- quality and decision gate records;
- lessons learned;
- improvement prioritisation;
- ownership assignments;
- change proposals;
- approval or rejection decisions;
- validation results.

## Completion Criteria

A Continuous Improvement review cycle is complete when:

- lifecycle performance has been reviewed;
- KPI have been evaluated;
- lessons learned are recorded;
- material improvement opportunities are identified;
- actions are prioritised;
- owners are assigned;
- required governance routes are identified;
- customer confidentiality is preserved;
- the Continuous Improvement Review Record is approved.

Continuous Improvement remains active where the engagement transitions into an
ongoing governance or implementation programme.

## Dependencies

Dependencies may include:

- availability of implementation feedback;
- customer participation;
- access to lifecycle KPI;
- framework governance capacity;
- change approval processes;
- confidentiality restrictions;
- availability of service owners;
- availability of architectural governance.

## Risks

Typical risks include:

- failure to capture lessons learned;
- unassigned improvement actions;
- informal framework changes;
- reuse of customer-confidential information;
- unsupported methodological change;
- repeated quality failures;
- improvement backlog without prioritisation;
- absence of outcome measurement;
- customer feedback treated as sole evidence;
- changes inconsistent with approved architecture;
- dependence on external AI-provider recommendations.

## Quality Gate

The Continuous Improvement Quality Gate verifies that:

- delivery performance has been reviewed;
- KPI and feedback have been evaluated;
- lessons learned are documented;
- improvement actions are prioritised;
- ownership is assigned;
- governance routes are respected;
- confidentiality is protected;
- architectural changes are escalated correctly;
- improvement results are measurable.

Failure of the quality gate requires review remediation, ownership assignment,
governance escalation or revision of the improvement plan.

# Lifecycle Integration

The Standard Delivery Lifecycle operates as a unified governance system.

No phase should be interpreted as an isolated procedural container.

Each phase contributes to a continuous chain connecting:

- customer objectives;
- scope;
- stakeholders;
- evidence;
- assessment criteria;
- findings;
- analytical conclusions;
- recommendations;
- executive decisions;
- deliverables;
- implementation actions;
- measurable outcomes;
- improvement records.

## Traceability Chain

Every material recommendation must be traceable through the following chain:

1. customer objective;
2. approved scope;
3. evidence source;
4. evidence item;
5. assessment criterion;
6. finding;
7. analytical conclusion;
8. recommendation;
9. executive decision;
10. accountable owner;
11. implementation action;
12. KPI or success measure;
13. observed outcome;
14. improvement decision.

Where any mandatory link is absent, the traceability limitation must be
documented and governed.

## Controlled Iteration

The lifecycle permits controlled return to an earlier phase when:

- new evidence becomes available;
- evidence reliability changes;
- material assessment errors are identified;
- analytical conclusions are challenged successfully;
- recommendations prove infeasible;
- executive validation reveals material context changes;
- reporting identifies unresolved inconsistencies;
- delivery acceptance is conditional;
- implementation feedback requires reassessment.

A return to an earlier phase must record:

- reason for iteration;
- affected outputs;
- decision authority;
- required remediation;
- responsible owner;
- expected completion condition;
- downstream impact.

Iteration must not erase prior records.

Superseded outputs must remain identifiable through version control and decision
traceability.

## Lifecycle Scaling

The lifecycle may be scaled according to:

- engagement size;
- organisational complexity;
- regulatory exposure;
- strategic significance;
- evidence volume;
- stakeholder complexity;
- implementation risk;
- service maturity;
- delivery duration.

Scaling may reduce the volume or formality of artefacts where proportionate.

Scaling must not eliminate:

- accountable ownership;
- approved scope;
- evidence registration;
- assessment traceability;
- documented findings;
- documented decisions;
- quality gates;
- completion criteria;
- final acceptance;
- lessons learned.

## Lifecycle Suspension

An engagement may be suspended when:

- required evidence is unavailable;
- executive sponsorship is withdrawn;
- decision authorities are unavailable;
- confidentiality or security cannot be maintained;
- material conflicts of interest arise;
- scope becomes ungovernable;
- legal or regulatory restrictions prevent continuation;
- methodological integrity cannot be preserved;
- customer actions invalidate required entry conditions.

Suspension must be documented through a formal decision record.

The record must identify:

- suspension reason;
- affected phase;
- unresolved conditions;
- retained evidence and artefacts;
- accountable decision authority;
- restart criteria;
- escalation route;
- closure conditions.

## Lifecycle Termination

Termination may occur when:

- the engagement objective is no longer valid;
- required prerequisites cannot be satisfied;
- material governance conditions are rejected;
- continuation would produce unreliable conclusions;
- contractual or legal conditions require termination;
- the customer formally withdraws;
- Cognitive Logic determines that methodological independence cannot be
  maintained.

Termination must preserve:

- the authoritative delivery record;
- completed evidence records;
- approved outputs;
- decisions;
- confidentiality controls;
- residual risk records;
- applicable retention requirements.

# Cross-Lifecycle Controls

## Evidence Control

Evidence must remain governed throughout the lifecycle.

Any evidence item used after Evidence Collection must retain:

- evidence ID;
- provenance;
- owner or custodian;
- acquisition date;
- reliability classification;
- access classification;
- scope relationship;
- assessment relationship;
- version or status.

Material evidence updates must trigger impact analysis.

## Decision Control

All material lifecycle decisions must be recorded.

A decision record must identify:

- decision ID;
- decision subject;
- decision authority;
- available options;
- supporting evidence;
- selected outcome;
- rationale;
- conditions;
- residual risk;
- effective date;
- follow-up actions.

Verbal decisions must be documented before they are treated as authoritative.

## Change Control

Changes affecting approved lifecycle outputs must be classified and governed.

Change categories include:

- scope change;
- evidence change;
- assessment change;
- analytical change;
- recommendation change;
- ownership change;
- priority change;
- reporting change;
- delivery change;
- framework change.

Material changes require:

- impact analysis;
- decision authority;
- version control;
- updated traceability;
- revalidation of affected outputs;
- updated quality gate status where applicable.

## Risk Control

Risks must be maintained throughout the lifecycle.

Each material risk must identify:

- risk ID;
- description;
- source;
- affected phase;
- probability;
- impact;
- severity;
- owner;
- mitigation;
- residual risk;
- status;
- escalation threshold.

Risks must not be considered closed solely because the engagement has moved to a
later phase.

## Exception Control

Any deviation from DF-002 must be recorded as a lifecycle exception.

An exception record must identify:

- requirement affected;
- reason for deviation;
- expected impact;
- compensating control;
- accountable owner;
- approval authority;
- duration;
- review date;
- closure condition.

Exceptions must remain visible in final reporting where they materially affect
confidence, conclusions, recommendations or residual risk.

## Quality Control

Quality control must operate at:

- activity level;
- deliverable level;
- phase level;
- lifecycle level.

Quality controls may include:

- completeness checks;
- consistency checks;
- evidence verification;
- methodological review;
- peer review;
- traceability review;
- editorial review;
- confidentiality review;
- approval verification;
- version-control verification.

Quality review evidence must be retained in the delivery record.

# Lifecycle KPI Model

Lifecycle KPI support delivery governance, performance measurement and
continuous improvement.

KPI must not be treated as substitutes for professional judgement.

They provide measurable evidence regarding execution quality, completeness,
timeliness, traceability and governance effectiveness.

## KPI Categories

Lifecycle KPI may be grouped into:

- engagement readiness;
- stakeholder participation;
- evidence completeness;
- evidence reliability;
- assessment coverage;
- assessment consistency;
- analytical traceability;
- recommendation actionability;
- executive decision completion;
- reporting quality;
- delivery acceptance;
- continuous improvement performance.

## KPI Requirements

Each KPI must define:

- KPI ID;
- name;
- purpose;
- calculation method;
- data source;
- measurement frequency;
- owner;
- target;
- threshold;
- reporting format;
- escalation condition.

KPI definitions must remain stable during an engagement unless a documented
change is approved.

## KPI Interpretation

KPI results must be interpreted in context.

A positive KPI result does not automatically demonstrate methodological quality.

For example:

- high evidence volume does not prove evidence relevance;
- complete assessment coverage does not prove scoring accuracy;
- high executive attendance does not prove decision quality;
- full recommendation ownership does not prove implementation feasibility;
- rapid delivery does not prove delivery completeness.

KPI must therefore be reviewed together with evidence quality, risks,
exceptions and professional judgement.

# Methodology Conformance

An engagement conforms to DF-002 when:

- all applicable phases have been addressed;
- entry conditions have been verified;
- mandatory activities have been performed;
- mandatory deliverables exist;
- phase responsibilities are assigned;
- material decisions are documented;
- required evidence is retained;
- KPI are measured;
- completion criteria are satisfied;
- quality gates are passed;
- deviations are governed through approved exceptions;
- final delivery and acceptance are documented;
- continuous improvement has been initiated where applicable.

## Conformance Levels

Conformance may be classified as:

- fully conformant;
- conformant with approved exceptions;
- partially conformant;
- non-conformant;
- not assessed.

A fully conformant engagement satisfies all applicable mandatory requirements.

Conformance with approved exceptions requires that every deviation has an
approved exception and compensating control.

Partial conformance indicates that one or more mandatory requirements remain
unresolved.

Non-conformance indicates material failure to apply the lifecycle or preserve
its governance controls.

## Conformance Assessment

Conformance assessment should evaluate:

- phase completeness;
- artefact completeness;
- evidence traceability;
- decision traceability;
- role assignment;
- quality gate status;
- risk governance;
- exception governance;
- version control;
- customer acceptance;
- improvement review.

Conformance results must be documented and retained.

## Non-Conformance

Material non-conformance may require:

- remediation;
- additional review;
- phase reopening;
- quality escalation;
- executive disclosure;
- delivery suspension;
- withdrawal of an affected output;
- framework governance review.

No engagement may be represented as fully conformant where material mandatory
requirements remain unresolved.

# Relationship with Enterprise Services

DF-002 applies horizontally across the Cognitive Logic Enterprise Service
Catalogue.

Each Enterprise Service specification defines:

- the business problem;
- service objectives;
- required inputs;
- service-specific activities;
- outputs;
- deliverables;
- KPI;
- evidence sources;
- dependencies;
- architectural constraints.

DF-002 defines how those service-specific elements are delivered through a
controlled operational lifecycle.

Where a service specification introduces additional lifecycle requirements,
those requirements become mandatory for that service.

Where a service specification is silent, DF-002 remains authoritative.

A service specification must not remove the minimum governance, evidence,
decision, quality or acceptance requirements established by DF-002.

# Relationship with DF-001

DF-001 — Enterprise Delivery Methodology defines the governing methodological
principles for Cognitive Logic Enterprise delivery.

DF-002 does not replace or reinterpret DF-001.

The relationship is:

- DF-001 defines the methodology;
- DF-002 defines the standard operational lifecycle;
- service specifications define service-specific execution requirements;
- future delivery procedures define detailed operational instructions;
- evidence standards define evidentiary controls;
- quality standards define detailed assurance controls;
- governance standards define decision and accountability structures.

Where ambiguity exists, DF-001 remains the methodological authority.

# Architectural Notes

The Standard Delivery Lifecycle is an integral component of the Cognitive Logic
Enterprise Delivery Framework.

It operationalises enterprise service delivery through governed phases,
evidence-based assessment, explainable analysis, traceable recommendations,
accountable decisions, controlled reporting and measurable improvement.

The lifecycle is technology-independent, vendor-independent and AI-provider
independent.

No external AI provider may act as:

- evidence authority;
- assessment authority;
- analytical authority;
- recommendation authority;
- validation authority;
- delivery decision authority.

The lifecycle is aligned with ADR-CLE-004 and the Cognitive Logic Sovereign
Intelligence Architecture.

Where computational support is used, governed outputs may derive exclusively
from approved Cognitive Logic capabilities, including:

- QEN Sovereign Engine;
- Governance Engine;
- Knowledge Graph;
- EVIDE;
- proprietary algorithms;
- intelligible data;
- verifiable evidence.

Computational support must remain:

- explainable;
- traceable;
- reviewable;
- evidence-linked;
- subordinate to accountable human governance.

Human accountability remains mandatory for:

- scope approval;
- evidence acceptance;
- assessment approval;
- analytical approval;
- recommendation approval;
- executive validation;
- final reporting approval;
- delivery acceptance;
- lifecycle closure;
- framework improvement.

DF-002 represents the authoritative operational lifecycle baseline for the
delivery of Cognitive Logic Enterprise Services.

Future revisions must be governed through approved change control, evidence,
quality review and architectural governance.

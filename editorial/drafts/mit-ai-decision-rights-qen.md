# Who Decides Is Not Enough: From AI Decision Rights to Verifiable Evidence

## The governance question

As artificial intelligence becomes embedded in organizational
decision-making, governance must address two distinct questions:

1. Who is authorized to make a decision?
2. What evidence makes that decision reconstructable and verifiable?

These questions are complementary, not interchangeable.

## The MIT contribution

MIT Sloan's article "A framework for determining when AI can make
decisions" discusses the allocation of decision rights between
humans and AI.

The framework considers decision ambiguity and the risk associated
with consequences.

This provides a useful perspective for determining the appropriate
role of AI in different decision contexts.

Primary references:

MIT Sloan:
https://mitsloan.mit.edu/ideas-made-to-matter/a-framework-determining-when-ai-can-make-decisions

MIT CISR:
https://cisr.mit.edu/publication/2026_0601_AIDecisionMatrix_SebastianWeillHaskampVomBrocke

The MIT CISR research distinguishes routine, consequential, exploratory and strategic decisions using ambiguity and risk. It also addresses framing, acting, learning, human accountability and organizational controls. The QEN proposal therefore does not claim that these governance elements are absent from MIT's work.

## The evidence-to-decision question

Allocating decision rights does not by itself establish that
an individual decision can be independently reconstructed.

A verifiable decision requires evidence concerning:

- the information available at decision time;
- the provenance and limitations of that information;
- the criteria applied;
- the role assigned to AI;
- the responsible human authority;
- the controls and exceptions recorded;
- the outcome and subsequent review.

## The QEN Sovereign perspective

Cognitive Logic develops QEN Sovereign as an evidence-to-decision
governance approach.

Its assessment sequence is:

DECLARED → OBSERVABLE → DOCUMENTED → VERIFIED

This sequence distinguishes organizational declarations from
observable facts, documentary support and verification outcomes.

The objective is not to replace the MIT decision-rights framework.

It is to investigate whether the evidence supporting a decision
is sufficiently traceable, documented and independently assessable.

## A complementary research hypothesis

Decision-rights allocation and evidence verification may be
combined into a broader governance architecture.

For example:

DECISION CONTEXT
→ AUTHORIZED DECISION RIGHTS
→ EVIDENCE PROVENANCE
→ APPLIED CRITERIA
→ HUMAN ACCOUNTABILITY
→ VERIFICATION
→ REVIEW

This is a proposed analytical integration, not a claim that MIT
has endorsed or validated QEN Sovereign.

## Practical implications

Organizations evaluating AI-assisted decisions should ask:

1. Which decisions can AI execute autonomously?
2. Which decisions require human authorization?
3. Which evidence supports each decision?
4. Can the evidence be reconstructed later?
5. Are exceptions and contradictions documented?
6. Who is accountable for the final outcome?

These questions can inform an evidence assessment without
assuming that documentation alone guarantees compliance.

## Conclusion

AI governance should address both the authority to decide
and the evidence supporting the decision.

Who decides matters.

What can be verified matters too.

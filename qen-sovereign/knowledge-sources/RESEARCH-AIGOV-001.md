## Executive Summary

Trustworthy AI systems require more than model performance.

As artificial intelligence moves toward autonomous and semi-autonomous agents, organizations need governance structures that make actions, evidence, responsibilities and decisions intelligible, attributable and reviewable.

AI Governance therefore depends on explainability, decision traceability, semantic identity, governed knowledge structures and human oversight.

The central question is not only what an AI system can do, but whether its actions remain understandable, attributable and governable.

## From model performance to system governance

Capability, accuracy, speed, scale and benchmark performance remain relevant measures of AI systems, but they are not sufficient for agentic systems.

A technically powerful system may still be difficult to audit, operationally unsafe or impossible to explain.

Governance requires that AI actions remain:

- intelligible;
- attributable;
- governable;
- reviewable.

## Agentic AI governance

AI agents may:

- interpret broad objectives;
- choose tools;
- retrieve external information;
- delegate subtasks;
- generate intermediate decisions.

This creates governance requirements including:

- clear identity for agents and system components;
- documented authority;
- operational boundaries;
- traceable evidence;
- traceable actions and decisions;
- human oversight for consequential operations;
- explainable relationships between inputs and outcomes;
- monitoring of dependencies and external tools.

## Explainability and decision traceability

Explainability alone is not sufficient when it only produces a plausible narrative about an AI output.

Governed decision traceability requires the ability to reconstruct:

- which evidence was used;
- which rules were applied;
- which component performed an action;
- who had authority to approve, reject or override an outcome.

A generated explanation is not equivalent to a governed decision trace.

## Knowledge architecture

Knowledge graphs, semantic models and governed information structures can connect:

- identities;
- evidence;
- policies;
- decisions;
- organizational responsibilities.

A governed knowledge architecture makes AI-supported decisions understandable, reviewable and verifiable.

## Semantic identity

Autonomous components should possess a recognizable semantic identity defining:

- what the component is;
- what it is authorized to do;
- which evidence it may access;
- which governance rules apply.

Semantic identity supports accountability in multi-agent environments.

Without clear identity and authority, multi-agent systems may become opaque chains of actions without clear ownership or responsibility.

## Human authority

AI governance must preserve meaningful human responsibility.

Consequential operations require human oversight and clear authority to approve, reject or override AI-supported outcomes.

Autonomy does not remove organizational accountability.

## Cognitive Logic approach

Cognitive Logic describes AI Governance, Knowledge Governance, Explainable AI, Semantic Identity and Decision Traceability as interconnected governance capabilities.

Within the QEN Framework, knowledge is treated as governed infrastructure supporting:

- evidence-based reasoning;
- transparent decisions;
- accountable AI systems.

## Claims boundary

This source argues that AI governance requires more than performance metrics and that trustworthy agentic systems require governance, traceability, identity, evidence and human oversight.

It does not establish that these elements alone guarantee trustworthy AI.

It does not provide a statutory compliance certification, legal determination or technical safety guarantee.

## Limitations

This publication is a Cognitive Logic research article presenting an architectural and governance perspective.

It does not constitute:

- legal advice;
- regulatory certification;
- accredited audit;
- technical safety certification;
- empirical proof that governance controls eliminate AI risk.

## Executive Summary

Europe does not have one AI gap. It has an alignment gap.

AI adoption, digital infrastructure, skills, institutions and governance are advancing at different speeds across the EU-27.

Strong infrastructure does not automatically produce enterprise AI adoption. High individual use of generative AI does not necessarily indicate organisational readiness, and mature digital public services do not eliminate shortages in skills, interoperability or governance capacity.

AI readiness should therefore be examined across separate dimensions rather than collapsed into a single synthetic score.

The framework considers:

- Enterprise AI Adoption;
- Social AI Adoption;
- Digital Foundations;
- Human & Institutional Capacity;
- Governance & Sovereignty Readiness.

The research does not assign a synthetic numerical governance score.

## EU baseline

In 2025, 20.0% of EU enterprises with at least 10 employees used artificial intelligence technologies.

At individual level, 32.7% of people aged 16–74 in the EU used generative AI tools.

In 2025, 52.7% of EU enterprises used paid cloud services.

These indicators describe different dimensions of readiness and should not be interpreted as a single league table.

A country may be advanced in cloud infrastructure while remaining weaker in enterprise AI adoption, skills or social adoption.

## European AI Readiness dimensions

Cognitive Logic analyses readiness through five separate dimensions.

### Enterprise AI Adoption

Organisational use of AI technologies.

### Social AI Adoption

Use of generative AI by individuals.

### Digital Foundations

Cloud, data analytics, connectivity and compute capacity.

### Human & Institutional Capacity

Digital skills, ICT specialists, public services and interoperability.

### Governance & Sovereignty Readiness

Governance capacity, strategic dependencies, cybersecurity, data control and human accountability.

No synthetic governance score is assigned.

Governance evidence is interpreted from official European Commission country reports and related institutional sources.

## Recurring European patterns

The EU-27 comparison identifies four recurring profiles.

### Frontrunners

Countries combining high adoption, strong foundations and mature institutional capacity while retaining specific vulnerabilities.

### Execution-gap countries

Countries with significant digital assets that do not fully convert those assets into enterprise adoption or organisational execution.

### Human-capacity gap countries

Countries where infrastructure or technology progress is constrained by skills and specialist availability.

### Structural-gap countries

Countries with simultaneous weaknesses across adoption, skills, enterprise digitalisation and institutional capacity.

These archetypes are Cognitive Logic interpretations and are not official European Union classifications.

## Italy — human-capacity and alignment gap

Italy's 2026 profile is not consistent with a uniformly digitally weak country.

Official European evidence indicates progress in:

- FTTP deployment;
- SME digitalisation;
- cloud;
- AI;
- data analytics;
- semiconductors;
- high-performance computing;
- quantum technologies;
- digital public services.

Persistent weaknesses remain in:

- basic digital skills;
- ICT specialist availability;
- parts of rural connectivity.

Italy's central challenge is increasingly one of alignment.

Digital assets and infrastructure are improving faster than the capacity of people and organisations to convert them into widespread, governed and productive AI use.

## Italian paradox

Three signals illustrate the mismatch.

### Cloud capacity

In 2025, 75.6% of Italian enterprises used paid cloud services.

### Individual GenAI use

19.9% of Italians aged 16–74 used generative AI tools, compared with 32.7% across the EU.

### Human capacity

European Commission reporting continues to identify shortages of ICT specialists and below-average digital skills as structural weaknesses.

The research therefore describes Italy's constraint as increasingly related to alignment and execution rather than infrastructure alone.

## AI readiness for SMEs

For smaller organisations, AI readiness is not equivalent to purchasing access to an AI tool.

Organisational readiness includes the ability to answer questions such as:

- Which AI systems are in use?
- Which data and sources do they depend on?
- Who owns the relevant process?
- Which decisions remain human?
- What evidence is retained?
- How can an output or decision be reconstructed and reviewed?

## Governed AI readiness

Digital maturity is not treated as a linear sequence in which infrastructure automatically produces adoption and adoption automatically produces effective governance.

The research frames readiness as an alignment across:

technology → data → skills → organisations → institutions → governance.

AI readiness emerges when these layers advance together rather than as isolated indicators.

The relevant question is therefore not only whether an organisation or country uses AI, but whether it can understand, control, document and verify how AI is being used.

## Claims boundary

The research supports the conclusion that AI readiness is multidimensional and that different European countries exhibit different combinations of adoption, infrastructure, skills, institutional capacity and governance readiness.

It does not establish a universal causal model in which one dimension automatically produces another.

It does not assign a synthetic numerical governance score.

It does not present Cognitive Logic country archetypes as official EU classifications.

It does not treat values from different years as directly interchangeable.

It does not claim greater precision than the underlying official evidence supports.

## Methodology and source-quality rules

The analysis covers the 27 EU Member States and uses official evidence available through August 2026.

Source-quality rules include:

- exact values explicitly published by Eurostat or the European Commission are treated as exact;
- values visually extracted from official charts are treated as approximations;
- country archetypes are Cognitive Logic interpretations;
- values from different years remain associated with their reference year;
- no synthetic numerical governance score is assigned.

Readiness should not be made artificially precise when the underlying evidence does not support that precision.

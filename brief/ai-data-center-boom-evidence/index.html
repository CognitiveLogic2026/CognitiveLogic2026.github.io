The AI Data Center Boom: What Can We Actually Verify?

Beyond investment announcements, electricity requests and employment promises: three perspectives on the infrastructure behind artificial intelligence.

November 25, 2026 · Infrastructure · Evidence Governance

From announcements to evidence

Artificial intelligence is usually discussed in terms of what machines can do. Increasingly, however, its future depends on electricity, land, construction, water, skilled workers and control over critical infrastructure.

Investment announcements suggest growth. Grid connection requests suggest demand. New facilities suggest technological progress.

But these indicators do not necessarily measure the outcomes they are used to describe.

Methodological note: the US employment evidence concerns data centers generally, not exclusively facilities dedicated to artificial intelligence.

01 · United States

What happens when construction ends?

Data centers can generate significant employment during construction. Their long-term operating requirements represent a different economic question.

A Virginia legislative review describes a representative 250,000-square-foot data center with approximately 50 full-time workers, including contractors. Industry representatives reported around 1,500 workers at peak construction.

These figures are illustrative, not a national employment ratio or a matched record of workers moving from construction to permanent employment.

Washington State's preliminary 2026 review of urban data center tax preferences identified nearly 300 reported construction and trade jobs associated with refurbishment projects in 2023 and 2024, alongside 53 reported new family-wage positions.

The employment requirements had not yet been independently verified. Actual wages were not collected in the annual reports.

Construction activity, permanent employment, local wages and public revenue must be evaluated separately.

02 · Italy

A grid request is not a data center

Requests for electricity connections indicate potential demand. They do not establish that corresponding facilities have been authorized, financed, constructed or brought into operation.

A credible assessment must distinguish requested capacity, approved connections, projects under construction, energized infrastructure and actual electricity consumption.

The relevant question is how much requested capacity will become operational, when, under what conditions and at whose expense.

03 · Europe

Location is not control

A data center located in Europe may provide employment, computing capacity and local investment. Physical location alone, however, cannot establish technological sovereignty.

Ownership, advanced computing hardware, software dependencies, contractual arrangements, operational authority and legal jurisdiction must be examined separately.

International partnerships can contribute to European computing capacity without necessarily undermining strategic autonomy.

The question requires evidence about control and dependencies, not geography alone.

04 · The common evidence gap

What does each indicator actually measure?

Investment announcements: declared commitments, not necessarily actual expenditure.

Construction employment: building activity, not necessarily permanent local jobs.

Grid connection requests: potential demand, not operational capacity.

Tax incentives: public support, not necessarily net economic benefit.

Facility location: geographic presence, not necessarily strategic control.

The problem arises when an indicator is used to support a conclusion that exceeds what the underlying evidence can demonstrate.

05 · Decision intelligence

What should public authorities measure?

Actual capital expenditure, permanent employment, local wages, contractor participation, operational electricity capacity, water consumption, fiscal outcomes and critical technology dependencies.

These measurements would not automatically establish whether a project is beneficial or harmful. They would make that judgment more transparent and accountable.

The Cognitive Logic perspective
What can an independent observer actually verify?

A promise is not an outcome. A request is not an operational facility. A location is not proof of control.

The real measure of the AI infrastructure boom will not be the scale of its announcements, but the quality of its documented outcomes.
